Reduced-Gravity Phase Separation

Undergraduate Researcher  |  Interphase Transport Phenomena Laboratory, Texas A&M Nuclear Engineering  |  Fall 2026 – Present  |  College Station, TX

On Earth, gravity separates gas from liquid for free: bubbles rise and liquid settles. In reduced gravity that stops happening, so fluid systems that have to work in space need another way to pull the two phases apart. Vortex phase separation spins the mixture so that rotation does the job gravity no longer can. I work on this research in Dr. Cable Kurwitz's lab, alongside graduate student Maximillian Jasek.

The Parabolic Flight Payload

The only way to see how these separators really behave in reduced gravity is to fly them. I'm redesigning the experiment payload for parabolic flight testing, which carries 8 to 10 test modules that each measure a different application of vortex gas-liquid separation. Alongside the payload, I design and verify the tests and the test equipment that go into it.

A parabolic flight only gives short windows of reduced gravity, so the payload has to be compact, secure, and ready to record the moment each window opens. That makes it as much a packaging and reliability problem as a fluids one.

Building the Hardware

I manufacture fixtures and test hardware in-house using CNC machining, 3D printing, and welding. It's the same loop I ran at Rochester Sensors, from design to machined part to verified fit, applied to experimental hardware instead of production parts.

Carbon Capture

The team is also applying the same phase separation research to carbon capture: a model that pulls CO2 out of air by cycling it with an amine solvent through multiple separation stages in series, integrated with a Rankine cycle.
